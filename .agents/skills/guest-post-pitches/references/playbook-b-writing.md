# Part B. Writing the pitch or article

Run Part B for every PURSUE site. Each draft must be ready to send as it stands: compelling, meeting every stated requirement, with nothing around it but the Send to and Guidelines page lines in the Doc. Part C decides where each finished pitch or article goes (outreach tool, Doc, or the Full Articles tab). Nothing in a draft is marked for replacement: any detail only Claire would know is invented and stated plainly, with no bold, brackets, quotes or flags (see Step B5).

The single biggest quality risk in Part B is a generic pitch: a polite email proposing a plausible-sounding listicle that an editor has seen a hundred times. Everything in Step B3 exists to prevent that. A pitch that follows every rule but has a forgettable idea has failed. The second biggest risk is a pitch that reads like a compliance form, where every stated requirement is echoed back, the site's process is announced, or limits are marked with their counts. The third is a pitch where Claire talks about herself for too long: two paragraphs of blog description and credentials push the idea down the page and read as self-congratulation. See "The about-Claire paragraph".

## What a pitch section never contains

The research behind a pitch (rules, example posts, angle reasoning, notes to the human) stays in the triage record and never appears in the drafts Doc. A pitch section must not have any of these headings or sections:

- "Rules quoted" or any list of the site's rules
- "Examples found" or example post links
- "Angle" or an angle justification
- A separate "Author bio" or "Suggested bio" section (see Step B4 for where a bio goes)
- "Needs human input"
- "Placeholders to replace" or any list of invented details

Anything a human must know that does not fit in the pitch itself (a language question, a required headshot field, a fee) goes in the tracker's `notes` column, not in the Doc. The research and checks still happen in full. They are just not in the Doc. (The reviewer note pushed with an email draft in Part C is the one place where the site's requirements and a short angle line travel with the pitch. It is never part of the email body. Article-first sites have no reviewer note, so their requirements go in the Full Articles tab instead, see Part C.)

After each batch, the chat reply carries what the Doc no longer does, briefly and in prose: which sites need a full article or real photos, any language, author-image or route flags.

## Meet the rules without reciting them

The research, the rule checking and the angle work all still happen in full. What changes is what appears in the pitch the editor reads.

- Never pile up specifics only to prove you read the guidelines. Do not list pixel widths, file sizes, file formats, photo counts per item, what you will not include (no zoos, no listicles, no hotel reviews), or how the draft will be delivered. A real writer does not say "All photos are mine, one per item plus a header, at 1600 pixels wide and under 300 kilobytes." That belongs in the human's head at the time of submission, not in the pitch.
- **Never announce the process the site imposes.** Many sites want a pitch, topic ideas, a query or an assignment before any article. When they do, the email is simply the pitch. Do not write "I'd like to request an assignment", "before I write anything", "as your guidelines ask", "per your submission process", "I understand you want pitches first", "this is a pitch, not a finished article" or any other sentence whose only purpose is to show the process was read. At most one short, natural clause at the end of the idea paragraph may tell the editor no article is attached ("I wanted to check that it suits you before I start", "I can send the full draft if it fits"). Never use editor jargon such as "assignment request", "commission" or "on spec" about the pitch itself.
- **Never list deliverable specs.** Do not mention headings, tables, internal links, royalty-free or original images, formats, delivery method, or a quick table of anything, even when the guidelines require them. They are met when the article is written. In a pitch the only spec that may appear is the rough length, as one natural phrase ("About 1,200 words.").
- **Open with a reason, not with the process.** The first lines show a specific post or section the pitch is answering and what it leaves out, or go straight to the idea. They never describe how the pitch will be handled.
- **The "would a person type this?" test.** Read each sentence and ask whether a real writer would type it unprompted to a stranger. If it exists only because a rule exists, cut it. Example of failure: "I'd like to request an assignment for your Bangkok section before I write anything. ... About 1,200 words with headings, a quick table of dishes and prices, internal links to your Wat Benchamabophit and other Bangkok posts, and royalty-free images." Example of the fix: "Your Wat Benchamabophit post got me thinking about the other side of Bangkok, the districts where people spend their days in meetings. I'd like to write a guide for them, and I wanted to check that it suits you before I start. ... About 1,200 words."
- Include a practical point only when the editor needs it to decide: the rough length, that the piece is original and not published elsewhere, and, only if the site asks, one short clause about photos or links. One natural sentence is the limit. Two or three of these points in a single closing sentence is already too many.
- Where the site has a form with specific fields, fill each field with what it asks for and nothing else. Specs are applied when the piece is delivered, not described in the pitch.
- **Never mark word counts or limits inside the text.** When a site sets a length for a summary, bio, pitch or article (for example a 100 to 150 word summary, a 50 to 100 word bio, 800 to 1,500 words), hit that length silently. Do not put a count after the text ("(128 words)"), in a field label ("Author bio (50 words):"), in a heading, or anywhere else as a sign of compliance. Count the words yourself with a script and fix the text until it fits. The only place a length may appear is as one natural phrase about the proposed piece when the editor needs it to decide ("about 1,200 words"). Field labels use the site's own wording and nothing more ("Author bio:").
- A rule you cannot comply with is a reason to change the idea, not a reason to explain it away in the email.
- Test: could the same sentence appear in a pitch for any site with the numbers swapped? If yes, cut it. Read the draft as the editor would. Anything that sounds like a checklist being ticked off goes.

## Links and commercial status

The only link that matters in any pitch is hotelhottubs.com (https://hotelhottubs.com). We can never promise that a link is not commercial, so the pitch never says it is.

- Never state, promise or imply that the link, the blog, the article or the pitch is non-commercial, unsponsored, unpaid-for, free of affiliate links, free of promotion, or that Claire is "not selling anything", "not a company", "not pitching for a client" or "not writing on behalf of a company". None of these statements may appear, in any wording ("non-commercial blog link", "no affiliate links", "not sponsored", "purely editorial", "no strings attached").
- If the guidelines say commercial, promotional, sponsored or affiliate links are not accepted, or that companies, brands or SEO agencies are not accepted, the pitch does not acknowledge it. It does not promise compliance, it does not argue that the link is fine, and it does not deny anything. It simply continues as a normal pitch. The rule is recorded in the triage record and in the reviewer note (see Part C) so Kai can see it and decide, and the draft is still written and pushed.
- Saying the piece is original, unpublished and written for that site only is fine. Saying it is free (no fee asked) is fine when the site's route is unpaid, because it is true. Describing the blog by what Claire writes there is fine.
- Do not add other links, brand names or promotion. One link to hotelhottubs.com, placed where the site allows it (the mention, the bio, or the link slot). If the site bans links in the message, keep the mention and drop the URL.

## Claire is a blogger, not a company

Hotel Hot Tubs is described as Claire's personal blog and side project, and Claire as an actual blogger writing about her own trips, because that is how she describes it. These rules govern tone and word choice only. They never license a statement about commercial status (see "Links and commercial status"): the pitch describes the blog plainly and does not assure the editor of anything about whether it or its link is commercial.

- Call it "my blog", "the blog I write" or "a side project", never "my company", "our site", "our platform", "the business", "the brand" or "the directory".
- Write in the first person singular. Never "we", "our team" or "our readers".
- Never use business words about Hotel Hot Tubs or the pitch: founder, CEO, owner, team, brand, company, business, platform, clients, partners, partnership, sponsorship, sponsored, budget, campaign, promote, exposure, backlink, SEO, audience growth, traffic, listings. (The one exception: when the site's own form asks for a backlink URL, answer the field in its wording.)
- Her marketing career is her day job. It is the reason she writes clearly. It is fine to say so, and to lead with it when writing ability is the point, but never as a reason the blog is a business.
- Describe the blog by what she writes there (hotels she has stayed in, the trips around them, what she ate), not by what it offers.
- Never offer payment, ask about rates, or ask about sponsored or paid placement. If a site's only route for companies is paid, that is a triage verdict, not something to raise in a pitch.
- Sign-off: see the Sign-off section. No name, title or company line after it.

## STRICT REQUIREMENT: the about-Claire paragraph

Every pitch must contain one short paragraph about Claire that does three jobs: it says what her blog hotelhottubs.com is, it gives the editor a reason to trust her as a writer, and it shows how she travels with one specific detail tied to the idea. This is not optional. It is also not two paragraphs. A blog description in one paragraph followed by a credentials paragraph in the next reads as too long and too self-congratulating, and is not allowed.

**Shape and length.** One paragraph, 2 to 3 sentences, about 50 to 75 words in total. Each sentence does one job, in this order or a reordering of it that reads naturally:

1. **The writer sentence.** Who she is as a writer, with the blog named and described as a side project and linked: hotelhottubs.com, a small side blog about U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite. This sentence may open with the writing credential (her marketing career turning complicated products into pages people finish reading) when writing ability is the point of the pitch, with the blog following in the same sentence.
2. **The credential sentence, only if it is not already in sentence 1.** One writing credential, the one that best supports this idea: her marketing career, writing for work, years of notes on her stays, or her hostel and work-travel years. One credential, not a list. If the first sentence already carries a credential, this sentence is the detail in point 3.
3. **The specific detail.** One concrete, checkable-sounding moment that shows how she travels and ties to this idea: a place, a meal, a trip. A moment beats an adjective every time ("On my last Nashville trip I skipped Broadway entirely and still ate better than in most cities" beats "I am a passionate foodie"). If invented, state it plainly as fact (Step B5).

**What the paragraph never does.**
- It never tells the origin story of the blog at length. "I started it because finding a room was a chore" is allowed only as a short clause and only when it serves the idea. Do not describe why she likes a soak after a work trip or on a weekend away with her husband unless the idea is about exactly that.
- It never stacks credentials (career, then hostel days, then foodie, then husband). Pick the one or two that serve this idea and leave the rest of the profile unused. Vary which ones lead from site to site.
- It never uses adjectives or claims about herself ("seasoned", "experienced", "avid", "passionate"). Show it with the detail, do not state it.
- It is never split into a blog paragraph and a credentials paragraph.
- It does not invent figures such as years writing the blog or number of hotels reviewed. If a figure would help, leave the sentence out or use a vague but true phrasing.
- It adds no hotel recommendations. The blog is a credential, never the subject of the piece, and is not pitched as a product or a directory.
- It says nothing about commercial status (see "Links and commercial status"). The old "it earns me very little" line is no longer used by default. Leave it out.

**The credential must make her a believable writer.** The editor is deciding whether to trust an unknown person with a slot. The paragraph has to answer "can she write?" and "does she know this subject?" in the fewest words. Do not skip the writing credential to save space. Compress it.

**Where a bio is requested.** When the site's guidelines explicitly ask for a bio, the bio (Step B4, point 7) carries the blog description and the writing credential. The about-Claire paragraph in the body is then reduced to one sentence: the specific traveler detail tied to the idea, with the hotelhottubs.com link if the site allows a link in the body. The blog is still described exactly once in the email.

**Other places the rule applies.** The email pitch, the contact-form message, the "about you" answers in structured forms, applications, and the cover note that goes with a full article all carry the paragraph, sized to the space. Where a field has a hard character limit, compress to one sentence that still says what the blog is, that it is a side project, and carries the credential.

Approved examples (use as the quality bar for length and shape, never as text to reuse). Details in them are invented examples, and a real pitch states its own invented details just as plainly (Step B5):

> Go World Travel (writing ability leads, idea about eating well): By day I write marketing for a tech company, where I've spent years turning complicated products into pages people actually finish reading. On evenings and weekends I run hotelhottubs.com, a small blog about U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite. On my last Nashville trip I skipped Broadway entirely and still ate better than in most cities.

> Offshore Travel Magazine (narrative-first): I write marketing for tech companies by day and keep a small side blog, hotelhottubs.com, about U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite. Years of hostels and work trips taught me to notice the small things, like the one table in a cafe where the owner's family eats.

> Travel Begins at 40 (very short word limit): I'm a mid-career marketer who writes hotelhottubs.com on the side, a blog about U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite, and I still plan every trip around where I'll eat.

> Trip Tradition (long article): My day job is marketing, but most of my travel writing happens on evenings and weekends for hotelhottubs.com, a small blog about U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite. I take notes on every stay, which is how I can write a long, detailed piece without padding it.

> Too long, do not write this (two paragraphs, stacked credentials, origin story): "I run hotelhottubs.com. It's a guide to U.S. hotels with an in-room hot tub or Jacuzzi suite, which I started on the side because finding one was a real chore, and I like a soak after a work trip or on a weekend away with my husband. My career has been in marketing for tech companies, so I've spent years making useful information easy to read. I've traveled since my hostel days and plan most trips around food. On my last Nashville trip I skipped Broadway entirely and still ate better than I have in most cities."

Rules:
- The link to hotelhottubs.com goes where the site allows (the paragraph itself, the in-pitch bio, or the link slot). If the site bans links in the message, keep the paragraph and drop the URL.
- Write the name out as "Hotel Hot Tubs" or "hotelhottubs.com". Never use the abbreviation "HHT" in anything sent to a site.
- Final check before delivering any pitch: find the about-Claire paragraph. If it is missing, or it is longer than about 75 words, or it runs to a second paragraph, or it holds more than one or two credentials, the pitch is not done.

## Kai's writing style, in a professional register (applies to every pitch, note, bio and article)

Extracted from how Kai writes in chat, then adjusted for a professional email to an editor. It must read like a real person wrote it: direct and plain, but polite and professional. Not like an AI, a marketing team, or a chat message. Claire's voice is this voice.

**Do:**
- Be direct. Lead with the point. Say what you want in the first or second sentence.
- Use short, plain sentences in complete form. A fragment is acceptable only rarely.
- Use contractions in moderation (I'm, it's, don't).
- Use plain words. "Started" not "launched". "Trip" not "journey". "Hard to find" not "challenging".
- Be opinionated and commit. Say "this is a gap" not "this might possibly be a gap". At most one "I think" per piece.
- Give simple reasons: claim, then why. ("Your readers skip the usual hotspots. This one fits.")
- Use parentheses for asides, the way Kai does. Sparingly. Never for word counts or other compliance marks.
- Be specific about the idea: name the town, the dish, the post title, the working title. Be specific about the story, not about administrative details.
- Every greeting addresses someone. Use the editor's first name when the site gives it ("Hi Mollie,"). When no name is known, address the site's team by the site's name ("Hi A Tiny Trip team,"). Never a bare "Hi," or "Hello,", and never "Dear Editor", "Hi there" or "To whom it may concern". Close with a short, courteous line and a thank you, then "Best regards," (see the Sign-off section).
- Keep a casual, friend-to-friend tone only where the site explicitly requires it for the article itself. The pitch email stays professional.

**Don't:**
- No abbreviations, acronyms or chat shorthand anywhere in text sent to a site. Banned examples: lmk, IMO, e.g., i.e., BL, HHT, OTA, ASAP, FYI, btw. Write them out ("let me know", "for example", "Hotel Hot Tubs"). Standard country, state and unit abbreviations in context (U.S.) are fine.
- No em dashes. No exclamation marks. Few semicolons.
- No slang or filler ("a pain", "a big fan of" is acceptable once, "Okay," as an opener is not, except inside a sample article for a site that requires a casual tone).
- No flowery or corporate language. Banned: delve, tapestry, vibrant, nestled, hidden gem, immerse, elevate, unlock, leverage, curated, embark, bucket list, "passionate about" (show it instead), "I hope this finds you well", "I'd love to", "excited to", "reach out", "collaborate", "synergy".
- No stacked hedges ("I was wondering if perhaps you might").
- No throat-clearing opener, no flattery paragraph. One specific line showing you read the site is enough.
- No lists of adjectives. No three-part rhythm for its own sake.
- No self-congratulation: no adjectives or claims about her own skill or experience, no stacked credentials, and no second paragraph about herself (see "The about-Claire paragraph").
- No compliance echo and no compliance marks: no word counts after text, no limits in labels, no announcing of the site's process, no list of deliverable specs (see "Meet the rules without reciting them"), and no assurances about commercial status (see "Links and commercial status").
- No editor jargon about the pitch itself ("request an assignment", "commission", "on spec").
- Don't explain the pitch back to the editor. Say it once.

**Test before delivering:** read it as the editor would. Would a busy professional be comfortable receiving it? Is it still clearly one person's plain voice? Would a real writer type each sentence unprompted? Is the part about Claire short enough that the editor reaches the idea quickly and still believes she can write it? Cut every sentence that sounds like a press release, a checklist or a process statement. Search for abbreviations, em dashes, exclamation marks, parenthetical word counts, process announcements and any assurance about commercial status.

## Sign-off

Every email, and every form message written in the form of an email, ends with the closing line "Best regards," and nothing after it. Do not add a name, title, company or link below it. The signature is added inside Gmail, and the outreach tool appends the inbox signature. Structured form fields (name, title, answers) take no sign-off.

## Language

All pitches, form answers, bios and notes are written in English, always. If the site's guest section is in another language, still pitch in English. Record the language question in the tracker's `notes` column (so a human can decide on a translation or ask the editor), and do not draft the pitch in that language.

## Photos and the author image

- Mention photos only when the site's guidelines explicitly require or request them, and then in one short natural clause with no specs (no counts, sizes or formats). When the guidelines say nothing about photos, do not mention photos in the pitch at all. Meet the exact count, size and format when the piece itself is delivered, and for full articles add the photo list the site requires.
- Never say that a photo, headshot or profile picture of Claire is attached or included. Any bio is text only. If a form has a required image field for the author, flag it in the tracker's `notes` column and leave it out of the pitch text.

## Non-negotiable pitch criteria

1. **Follow stated pitch or article criteria 100%.** See the submission mechanism step below. Follow them without reciting them, announcing them or marking them.
2. **Every pitch needs a specific, surprising angle** that passes the angle test in Step B3, and fits the site geographically (a region the site covers or lacks), philosophically (its values and voice) and practically (its format, length and stated rules). It must read like a high-quality article from a seasoned traveler that the site's readers would genuinely want. It must never feel like a thinly veiled marketing ploy or an affiliate article. The article does not need to mention hot tubs or Jacuzzis at all. The Hotel Hot Tubs link belongs in the about-Claire paragraph, the in-pitch bio, or the link slot the site allows.
3. **Study the site before writing.** Before drafting, look extensively for examples of writing on the site, particularly guest posts. Extra credit when the owner or webmaster calls out examples of good guest posts. Read at least two published guest posts or, where none exist, two recent posts, and note length, structure, tone, author bio format, link style, and how they cover food and lodging. Also check what the site has already covered (search its archive for the destination and the idea), so the angle fills a real gap. Name in the pitch the specific post you read and what it leaves out. This research shapes the pitch but is not written into the Doc.
4. **Write in English** (see the language section).
5. **Include the about-Claire paragraph** (one short paragraph, see the strict requirement above) and write in Kai's style, professional register, with no abbreviations.
6. **Mention photos only when the guidelines require them, never mention a headshot** (see the photos section).
7. **Never assure the editor about commercial status** (see "Links and commercial status").
8. **Never write a pitch for an Indian or Pakistani site** (see Step 0). If Part B turns up a clear India or Pakistan base for a site that passed Step 0, stop, change the verdict to SKIP_REGION_EXCLUDED and drop the draft.

## Step B1. Detect the submission mechanism and stage

The deliverable depends on how the site takes submissions. Record it in `submission_mechanism`, and record the stage in `submission_stage` and, for article-first sites, the delivery in `article_delivery`:

- **pitch-first**: the site wants a pitch, query, topic ideas, an assignment request or an introduction before any article is written or sent, and does not ask for the finished article with it.
- **article-first**: the finished article is part of the first thing sent. This is true however the site wants it delivered:
  - pasted into the email text (the article is the body of the email, or sits below a short cover note in the same email);
  - attached to the email as a document (Word, .doc, .docx, PDF, or similar);
  - linked from the email as a shared document (Google Doc, Dropbox, or similar);
  - submitted through a form field or upload.
  Typical wording: "submit your finished article", "send the completed post", "paste your article in the body of the email", "attach your article as a Word document", "we do not accept pitches", "include your full draft with your submission", "send us your post with photos". If a site accepts either a pitch or a finished article and does not require the article, treat it as pitch-first. If a site asks for a pitch and only later the article, it is pitch-first. If a site requires the draft or sample article together with the pitch, it is article-first.

| Mechanism | Examples | Stage | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email pitch | Contact email for pitches | pitch-first | Pitch email (subject and body, ending "Best regards,"), with a bio inside it only when the guidelines ask for one |
| Contact form | A generic form | pitch-first | Pitch message sized to the form, ending "Best regards,", with a bio inside it only when the guidelines ask for one |
| Structured pitch form | Fields for titles, image links, previous work | pitch-first | Form-ready answers for every field, with 2 to 3 title options |
| Finished-article form | Form that takes the full article and photos, "not pitches" | article-first | The full article (see below), the form fields, and the photo list |
| Finished-article email, article in the email text | The article is pasted into the body of the email | article-first | The full article with a short cover note above it, and the photo list |
| Finished-article email, article attached or linked | The article goes as an attached document or a shared-document link | article-first | The full article as the document text, a short cover note for the email, and the photo list |
| Application | Apply to be a contributor | pitch-first | Application text plus a sample idea |

When a site requires a full article, write the full article to the stated word count and structure. Invented details are stated plainly per Step B5. Note "full article required" in the tracker's `notes` column. Do not add a "needs human input" section to the Doc. Article-first sites are routed separately from pitches, whatever the delivery method: see Part C, Step C2.

## Step B2. Extract and check the rules

Collect every stated rule (word count, photos, links, bio, topics, tone, exclusivity, response time, what is rejected) into the triage record. After drafting, check the draft against each rule and fix anything that does not comply, except that rules about commercial links, sponsorship, affiliates and companies are recorded but not answered in the text (see "Links and commercial status"). Do not break a rule even when it would help the pitch (for example, if hotel reviews are banned, include none). For summaries, bios and full articles, count words and paragraphs programmatically against the stated minimums and maximums, and re-check after every edit. The counts stay in your checking, never in the text. The rules are not copied into the Doc.

## Step B3. Choose the angle (the step that decides quality)

Pick one angle and justify it to yourself in 2 to 3 lines: the gap it fills, why this site's readers would want it, and why Claire is the right person to write it. Record it in `suggested_angle` in the triage record only.

**Do not start from the generic version of the topic.** "Best things to do in X", "A food lover's guide to X", "Hidden gems of X" and "Why you should visit X" are the defaults an editor sees every day. Start instead from the one thing about the place or the reader that is surprising, tense, funny or counterintuitive, and build the piece around that. Examples of the difference:

- Generic: a babymoon destination guide. Specific: ten questions to ask a hotel before you book a babymoon (the room, not the destination).
- Generic: a Nashville weekend list. Specific: weekend getaways from Nashville to Tennessee's utopian experiments (a colony, a commune, a secret city).
- Generic: a Turkey food guide. Specific: a Turkish breakfast field guide organized around one argument (onion in the menemen or not).
- Generic: a Belize lodge guide. Specific: the reluctant partner's guide to a jungle lodge.
- Generic: a Portugal scouting guide. Specific: scout in January, the worst month, and see what summer hides.

**The angle test.** An angle passes only if all of these are true:

1. It can be stated in one sentence that makes an editor want the rest (a tension, a number, a rule, a twist or a question), not just a topic.
2. It is not a destination plus a generic format. There is a reason for the piece that is not "readers like lists about this place".
3. The site's own archive does not already have it. The pitch names the specific post you read and what it leaves out.
4. A reader who has never heard of Claire would still want it. Her background is the reason she can write it, not the reason it exists.
5. It could not be pasted unchanged into a pitch for a different site. If it could, it is too generic.

If an idea fails the test, discard it and generate another. Generate at least three candidate angles per site before choosing.

**Angle patterns that work** (a menu to draw from, not to repeat): a counterintuitive rule or method; a then-and-now comparison on the same route; a niche audience the site ignores (the reluctant partner, the solo diner, the car-free traveler, the vegetarian on a lamb tour); a booking-page translation guide (what listing phrases really mean); a hidden second history behind a famous place; an organizing constraint (one food, one fare, one road, one season, one hour); a reflection triggered by a small habit change; a reported feature on something every traveler notices and nobody explains; a repeatable series with a first installment; a practical checklist for a decision the reader is about to make.

**Variety across a batch.** Keep an angle ledger in the triage record: one line per pitch with the angle pattern, the destination, the credential used and the specific detail used in the about-Claire paragraph. Before choosing an angle, read the ledger and do not reuse a pattern, destination, dish, credential lead or traveler detail more than twice in a batch. Do not default to food for every site: use it where it truly fits and vary the strengths you draw on (work travel, hostel years, hotel stays seen from the booking side, slow travel, reading details closely, her marketing background, couple travel). Avoid angles that merely restate what the Hotel Hot Tubs blog already covers.

## Step B4. Write the draft

**Pitch email structure (130 to 220 words including any bio the site requires, in Kai's style, professional register).** These are the elements to include, not a template to fill in the same way each time. Vary the opening, the order of the middle paragraphs and the sentence rhythm from site to site so the batch does not read as one form letter.

1. Subject line that names the idea (the hook, not just the topic). Never "Assignment request" or similar process wording unless the site dictates the exact subject.
2. Greeting that addresses someone: "Hi <editor first name>," when the name is known, otherwise "Hi <Site name> team," (never a bare "Hi,"). Then one opening that shows you read their site (name a specific post or section by title) and what it leaves out, or goes straight to the idea. Where it fits, open on the hook or a short scene instead. The opening is never about the site's process.
3. The idea: working title, the hook, what the piece covers (a short outline when the site allows), the rough length as one natural phrase, and why their readers would like it. On pitch-first sites, one short natural clause may say no article is attached yet ("I wanted to check that it suits you before I start"). Nothing about headings, tables, links or images.
4. **The about-Claire paragraph (required):** one paragraph, 2 to 3 sentences, about 50 to 75 words, that describes the blog as a side project, gives one writing credential, and ends on one specific traveler detail tied to the idea, reworded for this site (see the strict requirement). Never a separate blog paragraph followed by a credentials paragraph. It sits after the idea, so the editor reads the idea first.
5. At most one natural sentence of practical points the editor needs to decide (original and unpublished, or one short clause about photos if the site asks). Do not recite rules or specs, and say nothing about commercial status.
6. **Author bio, only when the guidelines explicitly request one:** write it inside the email itself, as a short paragraph just before the close (for example starting "Here is a short bio:"), 2 to 3 sentences, text only, linking hotelhottubs.com in the format the site uses. When a bio is included, it carries the blog description and the credential and the about-Claire paragraph shrinks to one sentence (see the strict requirement). When the guidelines do not ask for a bio, there is no bio. Never put the bio in a separate section of the Doc.
7. A short, courteous close with thanks, then "Best regards," and nothing after it.

**Full article (article-first sites, whether it will go in the email text, as an attachment, as a link or through a form):** follow the site's structure to the letter (intro, headers, conclusion, word count), write in Claire's voice as a factual guide or story as the site prefers, include the required sections and links, include no banned content, add the photo list the site asks for (with the count and sizes it requires), and the form field answers. A story needs a scene, a turn and a specific detail, not a summary of a place. A bio goes in the form field or cover note the site specifies, not in a separate Doc section. The cover note carries the about-Claire paragraph in its short form (blog, credential, one specific detail, two or three sentences at most) and ends "Best regards,". Summaries, bios and titles inside a full article package carry no word counts or limits in their labels or after the text.

## Step B5. Invented details are stated plainly

When the pitch needs details only Claire knows (a trip, a restaurant, an anecdote), invent a specific, plausible detail consistent with her profile and state it as fact. Specific beats generic: a named market, a number, a small funny moment. Never bold it, bracket it, quote it or flag it anywhere, in the draft, the Doc or the reviewer note. Kai has decided the assumption is fine, so no draft ever carries a "to confirm" marker.

- Never invent anything that cannot be written as a plain assumption: URLs, social handles, email addresses, dates the reader could check, photo-folder links, or links to her past work other than the Ruthin Castle post and hotelhottubs.com. If a site asks for one of these, leave that sentence out; do not insert a placeholder.
- Facts about the site, quotes and statistics must come from fetched sources. Facts about places, history, prices and schedules that were not fetched are stated cautiously and generally.
- Honesty check on invented experience: never invent a claim that would be harmful or misleading, for example that Claire was pregnant, held a credential, or reported an interview that never happened. Where an angle depends on first-hand experience that would be harmful if false, pick a different angle.
- No "Placeholders to replace" list and no human-input line anywhere.

## Part B output

This is the format for everything that goes into the Doc: pitches that cannot be sent by email, and article-first packages (see Part C). For each such site produce one section, in this order and nothing more:

1. **Site heading** (H2, the domain or site name) with a checkbox, so the human can tick it off once sent. Default: Docs API `createParagraphBullets` with `BULLET_CHECKBOX` on the H2.
2. **Guidelines page:** a link to the site's guest-post or pitch guidelines page.
3. **Send to:** the contact email, or the submission form link, plus any required subject line. If the address is masked or obfuscated, decode it when readable (Cloudflare `data-cfemail`: XOR each byte with the first; "info at site dot com" becomes info@site.com). If no address can be found, link the exact page to get it from.
4. **The pitch** (subject line, then the email body ending "Best regards,"), or for an article-first site the cover note, the article and the form field answers. Any requested bio is inside the email.

No rules, examples, angle, separate bio, needs-human-input or placeholder sections (see "What a pitch section never contains").

Before finishing each section confirm: the angle passes the angle test, greeting addresses the editor by name or the site's team by name, the about-Claire paragraph is present as one paragraph of about 50 to 75 words, describes a personal blog and side project, carries one writing credential and one specific detail tied to the idea, and is not followed by a second paragraph about Claire, no business words about Hotel Hot Tubs and no "we", the email ends "Best regards," with no name or signature below it, Kai's style in a professional register, no abbreviations, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no compliance echo or spec recitation, no announcement of the site's process (no "request an assignment", no "before I write anything"), no list of deliverable specs (headings, tables, links, images), no word counts or limits written into the text or its labels, no assurance about commercial, sponsored or affiliate status, every sentence one a real writer would type unprompted, written in English, photos mentioned only where the guidelines require them and then in one short clause, no headshot mentioned, bio inside the pitch only when the guidelines ask for it, no bold, bracketed or flagged text anywhere, Guidelines page and Send to lines present, none of the banned sections present, every stated rule met, and the site is not based in or run from India or Pakistan.

## Where drafts go: three destinations

- **Outreach tool (Part C):** pitch-first sites with a readable email address. Not copied into the Doc.
- **Batch Google Doc:** pitches that cannot go by email (submission forms, structured pitch forms, contributor applications, and sites whose address is masked or could not be read), and every article-first package, whether the site wants the article pasted into the email text, attached, linked or submitted through a form. One new Google Doc per batch in the Drive folder "Guest Post Outreach", named "Guest Post Drafts - <batch>" (for example "Guest Post Drafts - C1-B1"). If a batch has nothing for the Doc, create no Doc. Do not create local files or Claude Docs as the deliverable, and do not rewrite earlier batch Docs unless asked.

**Paste-ready layout** (the human copies a pitch straight into an email):
- Every paragraph has space above and below set to 0 and line spacing 1.15. Email paragraphs are separated by one empty paragraph, like a typed email.
- The first hotelhottubs.com in each pitch is a real link to https://hotelhottubs.com, blue and underlined. The Send to and Guidelines page URLs are links too.

## Batch use

When given many domains, deduplicate by domain first, run the Step 0 region pre-screen on every domain and drop the excluded ones, then evaluate the rest independently and in parallel (subagents are fine). Return one combined table sorted by verdict (PURSUE first, then PAID_ONLY, NEEDS_REVIEW, SKIP) and, within PURSUE, by the discovery tool's overall score if one was provided. Finish with counts per verdict (SKIP_REGION_EXCLUDED is counted on its own line), a count of NEEDS_REVIEW rows by `review_reason`, a count of pitch-first versus article-first PURSUE rows, a list of domains where pitch_url differed from the supplied URL, a list of domains with `region_check = unclear` so Kai can decide, and a list of PURSUE rows with low confidence so a human can skim them. Push pitch-first email pitches to the outreach tool (Part C), write the rest, including every article-first package whatever its delivery method, into the batch's Google Doc with one section per site, add a Full Articles row for each article-first package, and flag every site where a full article is required in the tracker's `notes` column.

**Keep Part B fresh across a long batch.** Quality drops when many pitches are written in one long context. Write pitches in groups of about five to eight sites, and start each group with the angle ledger from the earlier groups (pasted in or passed to the subagent) so angles, destinations, credential leads and traveler details do not repeat. If you use subagents for Part B, give each one the ledger, the site's collected rules, the example posts read, the angle test from Step B3, the about-Claire paragraph rules (one paragraph, 2 to 3 sentences, about 50 to 75 words), the blogger-not-company rules, the links-and-commercial-status rule, the no-recitation, no-process-announcement, no-spec-list and no-word-count-marks rules, the plain-invented-details rule (Step B5) and the sign-off rule. After the last group, scan the whole set for repeated openings, repeated closings and repeated about-Claire paragraphs (the same credential lead or the same traveler detail in more than two pitches), and rewrite any that read like the same email.
