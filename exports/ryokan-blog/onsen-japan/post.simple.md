## What Is an Onsen? Its Meaning and What Qualifies

In Japan, an onsen (温泉) translates to "hot spring," but the term holds a specific legal meaning. Under the Hot Springs Act, water from the ground counts as an onsen if it comes out at 25°C or warmer, or if it contains enough of at least one of 19 listed substances, such as sulfur, iron or carbonic acid. So a spring can be cool and still qualify because of what's dissolved in it, which is why some onsen baths are reheated.

The word also covers the places built around the water. An onsen can be a single bathhouse, the bath inside a ryokan, or a whole town such as Kusatsu Onsen or Kinosaki Onsen, where the town name carries the word. Hokkaido has the most onsen areas of any prefecture (226). Oita Prefecture in Kyushu, where Beppu and Yufuin are located, has the highest count of individual spring sources, totaling 5,094.

## A step-by-step guide to enjoying an onsen

![Navy noren curtain marked 男 for men hanging over the entrance to a men's changing room](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Noren_%2845556489832%29.jpg/1920px-Noren_%2845556489832%29.jpg){w=360}
*The noren curtain indicates the gender: 男 for men, 女 for women.*

Onsen customs are built around a single principle: the hot water is communal, so every bather must enter clean and maintain a peaceful atmosphere.

1.  **Remove footwear** upon entering. When staying at a ryokan, you typically walk to the bathing area wearing the slippers and yukata provided in your guest room.
2.  **Identify the correct side.** Curtains, or noren, are typically colored blue or marked 男 for men, and red or marked 女 for women. Some establishments alternate which gender uses which bath at different times of day, so it's wise to verify each visit.
3.  **Take off all clothing** in the changing area, placing your items in a basket or locker. Do not wear a swimsuit. Bring only the provided small towel into the bathing hall.
4.  **Thoroughly wash before entering.** Using a stool at a washing station, clean your entire body and hair, ensuring all soap is completely rinsed away.
5.  **Enter the bath slowly and carefully.** The water temperature is high. Ease yourself in, keeping your head above the surface.
6.  **Prevent the towel from touching the water.** You can place the folded towel on your head or set it on the bath's edge. Long hair should be tied up to keep it out of the water.
7.  **Limit soaking sessions.** Initially, ten minutes is a sufficient duration. You can exit, cool off, and re-enter if desired.
8.  **Dry yourself before returning.** Use the small towel to remove excess water from your skin so you do not wet the floor in the changing room. Many people choose not to rinse after their final soak, allowing the mineral residue to remain.

## Onsen rules people ask about

Bathing in a hot spring follows a few key guidelines that ensure a respectful and pleasant experience for everyone. The most common queries are answered here.

- **Bathing suits are not permitted.** In gender-segregated communal pools, guests are expected to be unclothed. If you'd rather not, book a private bath (see below).
- **No phones or cameras in the changing room or bath.** The changing and bathing areas are private spaces for others.
- **Please keep the water calm.** Loud play, swimming, or diving is discouraged, and voices should be kept low.
- **Keep towels and soap out of the soaking pools.** Washcloths should not enter the water, and cleansing should happen at the washing stations.
- **Avoid entering the baths after heavy alcohol consumption.** It is also wise to hydrate with water both before and after a long soak.

## Onsen Bath Varieties

![Courtyard of Hyotan Onsen in Beppu with noren for the men's bath and the women's sand bath](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/19/Hyotan_Onsen_%285011201676%29.jpg/1920px-Hyotan_Onsen_%285011201676%29.jpg)
*Hyotan Onsen in Beppu, where the women's side includes a sand bath.*

- **Shared indoor bath (daiyokujo).** This is the primary bathing area, which is gender-segregated, found at any ryokan or public bathhouse.
- **Open-air bath (rotenburo).** This outdoor pool, typically built from rock or wood, is the classic image of an onsen experience.
- **Reservable private bath (kashikiri).** You can book this bath, like the 45-minute slots at Mount View Hakone, for exclusive use by your group. Access might be free or require a fee, depending on the inn.
- **In-room bath.** Your accommodation includes a private tub, located on a balcony or in the bathroom, allowing you to bathe on your own schedule.
- **Mixed-gender bath (konyoku).** Men and women share this bath, a practice now uncommon and mostly limited to historic mountain hot springs. Often, women are permitted to use a covering wrap.
- **Foot bath (ashiyu).** You'll find these shallow, complimentary pools for soaking feet in nearly every onsen town.

It's important to know that labels like "private" or "open-air" don't guarantee the water is from a natural hot spring, a common point of confusion for newcomers. At [Nishimuraya Honkan](https://ryokansociety.com/kinosaki-onsen/nishimuraya-honkan) in Kinosaki, the open-air baths in some rooms are heated plain water, because the town keeps its spring water for the shared baths. Meanwhile, at [Mount View Hakone](https://ryokansociety.com/hakone/mount-view-hakone), the outdoor baths are filled with milky sulfur water sourced from Owakudani, while indoor baths use heated groundwater. Always verify the water source if you are choosing a room specifically for its bath.

## Determining if a Bath Uses True Hot-Spring Water

Growing up in a hotel family in Tokyo, I learned early that the most honest description of a bath is usually the small sign by the changing-room door, written in Japanese. Most onsen post an analysis sheet (温泉分析書) and a note on how the water is handled. Four phrases tell you most of what you need.

| Sign Posted | How to Read It | Its Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 源泉掛け流し | Gensen kakenagashi | Spring water runs directly into the bath and drains away after use; it is never recycled. The best you can get. |
| 加水 | Kasui | Additional water, typically cold, is mixed in. This is often done to lower the temperature of a spring that emerges too hot. |
| 加温 | Kaon | Heat is applied to the water. This is common when the source emerges at a temperature lower than what is ideal for bathing. |
| 循環 | Junkan | The bath water passes through a filtration system and is pumped back in, similar to the operation of a swimming pool. |

You should not view any single one of these as automatically bad. A spring that requires heating still qualifies as a genuine onsen, and recirculation is a standard method for larger hotels to maintain hygiene in a sizable bath. However, if selecting your accommodation primarily for its bathing facilities, it is wise to inquire about their practice. Excellent ryokan operators are usually happy to explain. On our own inn listings, we include this information when provided, using descriptions like "free-flowing" or "partially supplemented."

## Onsen with tattoos: what actually works

Tattoos are still the hardest part of onsen in Japan. Many shared baths turn away anyone with visible ink, a rule rooted in tattoos' old link with organized crime. Our numbers show how it plays out: only [25 of our 2,598 ryokan](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?tattoos=fine) say tattoos are fine in their shared baths, while [1,331 let you bathe privately](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?tattoos=private), in a bath in your room or a private bath you book.

So for most travelers with tattoos, the answer is a private bath. Small tattoos can sometimes be covered with a skin-colored sticker, but that's the inn's call, not yours. Some whole towns are relaxed about it. Kinosaki's seven public bathhouses allow tattoos of any size, and both of our Hakone picks below allow tattoos, according to their pages.

How to ask: an inn's tattoo rule is often only on its Japanese website, if it's written down at all. When I book for friends, I send one short line before paying, with the size and where the tattoo is, and ask which baths they can use. A clear question gets a clear answer, and nobody is embarrassed at the bath door. If you want the shortest list, we have [8 ryokan with private baths where tattoos are fine everywhere](https://ryokansociety.com/private-onsen?tattoos=fine).

## Best Seasons and Soaking Tips

Onsen are open all year, but winter suits them best, with snow around the open-air baths in the mountains. Autumn leaves come second.

To escape crowds and higher rates, steer clear of major local holidays like New Year, Golden Week from late April into early May, and the Obon festival around mid-August. A weeknight is quieter and cheaper.

In the water, start with 5 to 10 minutes and cool off between rounds. Two or three short soaks beat one long one. At an inn, don't miss the after-dinner and early-morning baths.

## Onsen in Japan FAQ

**Do you have to be naked in an onsen?**
Yes, in shared baths. Swimsuits aren't worn, and you carry only a small towel that stays out of the water. If that's a dealbreaker, a [bath in your room](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?bath=room) or a private bath you book lets you bathe alone or with your own group.

**Are onsen mixed gender?**
Almost all are separated by gender. Mixed baths (konyoku) still exist, mostly at old mountain onsen, and many of those allow a bath wrap. Families and couples who want to bathe together usually book a [private bath](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?bath=private).

**How much does an onsen cost?**
Public bathhouses charge a small entry fee, and towels are often extra. At a ryokan the baths are included, and many small inns include their private baths too.

**Can children go in an onsen?**
Yes. Small children commonly enter the shared bath with a parent. The age limit for going in with the other parent is set locally, so ask at the front desk. Some ryokan set age limits for staying, so check the inn page, or start with [ryokan that welcome children](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?children=welcome).

**May I bathe during menstruation?**
As a courtesy to others, many avoid the shared baths on days with heavier flow. Using a private bath in your accommodation provides a simple alternative.

**Is there an onsen near Tokyo?**
Hakone and Kusatsu are the two classic trips, and both have dozens of ryokan with their own hot-spring baths. Discover additional options in our guide to [ryokan near Tokyo](https://ryokansociety.com/near-tokyo).

**Can I just visit for the day?**
Yes. Public bathhouses operate in onsen towns, and certain ryokan admit day guests to their facilities. The night and morning baths only come with a stay.
