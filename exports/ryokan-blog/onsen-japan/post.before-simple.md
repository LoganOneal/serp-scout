Among the [2,598 ryokan we list](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans), [1,700 have hot-spring water](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?onsen=yes) in some or all of their baths, so roughly a third of Japanese inns use standard heated water even when the bath looks the part.

## Onsen explained: meaning and what makes it one

Onsen (温泉) translates to 'hot spring,' and in Japan, it's legally defined. The Hot Springs Act states that groundwater qualifies as an onsen if it emerges at or above 25°C or contains sufficient levels of at least one of 19 designated elements, including sulfur, iron, or carbonic acid. This allows even cooler springs to qualify based on mineral content, which explains why some baths are warmed after extraction.

Onsen also means the places built around the water: this could be a single bathhouse, a bathing area within a ryokan, or an entire town like Kusatsu Onsen or Kinosaki Onsen, where the name itself includes the term. Hokkaido has more onsen areas than any other prefecture, with 226, while Oita in Kyushu, home to Beppu and Yufuin, has the highest number of springs at 5,094, according to the same Ministry of the Environment count.

## Using an onsen step by step

![Navy noren curtain marked 男 for men hanging over the entrance to a men's changing room](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Noren_%2845556489832%29.jpg/1920px-Noren_%2845556489832%29.jpg){w=360}
*This noren indicates which side is which: 男 for men, 女 for women.*

Onsen etiquette follows one core idea: since bathers share the same water, you must enter clean and keep it calm.

1. **Shoes off** before stepping inside. At a ryokan, you’ll walk to the bath wearing slippers and the yukata from your room.
2. **Find your side.** Noren typically hang in blue or with 男 for men, and red or with 女 for women. Since some inns reverse the bathing times between morning and evening, be sure to confirm again the following day.
3. **Undress completely** inside the changing area, leaving your clothes in a basket or locker. No swimsuits are worn. Bring just the small towel into the bath with you.
4. **Wash first, sitting down.** Take a seat on the stool at a shower station, clean your body and hair, then rinse away all soap completely.
5. **Get in slowly.** The water is hot. Ease yourself in, making sure your head stays above the surface.
6. **Keep the towel out of the water.** Place it folded on your head or set it aside on the rim. Make sure long hair is tied up and does not touch the bathwater either.
7. **Soak in short rounds.** Starting out, ten minutes per soak is sufficient. Exit, cool off, and re-enter later if desired.
8. **Dry off before the changing room.** Use the small towel to pat your body dry so the floor stays free of water. Skipping the last rinse is common among bathers who want to retain the water’s minerals on their skin.

## Commonly asked questions about onsen rules

- **No swimsuits.** Shared baths are nude and separated by gender. If that’s not for you, you can book a private bath instead.
- **No phones or cameras** allowed where guests change or bathe. People are not wearing clothes in these areas.
- **No swimming, splashing or diving.** Keep your voice low.
- **No towels or washcloths in the water,** and no soap should go into the tub either.
- **Skip the bath after drinking** a lot, and hydrate both before and after spending a long time in the water.

## Common bath types you'll encounter

![Courtyard of Hyotan Onsen in Beppu with noren for the men's bath and the women's sand bath](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/19/Hyotan_Onsen_%285011201676%29.jpg/1920px-Hyotan_Onsen_%285011201676%29.jpg)
*At Hyotan Onsen in Beppu, the women's section includes a sand bath.*

- **Shared indoor bath (daiyokujo).** This is the central bathing area at a ryokan or bathhouse, split by gender.
- **Open-air bath (rotenburo).** This is the image that comes to mind: a wooden or stone soaking pool set outside.
- **Private bath to book (kashikiri).** You reserve this bath for your group for a fixed time, like 45 minutes at Mount View Hakone. Some inns charge a fee; others offer it free when it's not in use.
- **Bath in your room.** It's a tub on your balcony or inside your bathroom, so you can soak anytime you want.
- **Mixed bath (konyoku).** Men and women share these baths together. They’re rare today, found mostly in old mountain onsen, and many allow women to wear a bath wrap.
- **Foot bath (ashiyu).** You’ll find these free public foot soaks in most onsen towns.

"Private" and "open-air" don't mean hot-spring water, which surprises a lot of first-timers. At [Nishimuraya Honkan](https://ryokansociety.com/kinosaki-onsen/nishimuraya-honkan) in Kinosaki, the open-air baths in some rooms are heated plain water, because the town keeps its spring water for the shared baths. At [Mount View Hakone](https://ryokansociety.com/hakone/mount-view-hakone), the open-air baths are milky sulfur water piped from Owakudani, while the indoor baths are heated groundwater. Check which bath is which before you pay extra for a room's tub.

## How to tell if bathwater is from a natural hot spring

Raised in a Tokyo hotel family, I learned that the most accurate details about a bath are often on the small Japanese sign near the changing-room entrance. Most onsen display a water analysis sheet (温泉分析書) and a note stating how the water is managed. Just four key phrases reveal most of what you need.

| On the sign | Reading | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 源泉掛け流し | gensen kakenagashi | Fresh spring water runs directly in and out; no recycling. The best you can get. |
| 加水 | kasui | Water gets added, usually to lower the temperature of a spring that comes out too hot. |
| 加温 | kaon | Heated water, typically when the source spring is below bathing temperature. |
| 循環 | junkan | Water is filtered and recirculated, similar to a swimming pool. |

None of these is a warning sign. Water from a heated spring still counts as onsen, and recirculated water is standard in larger hotels for keeping big baths clean. If you're choosing a room for its bathing setup, verify which system is used. Good ryokan hosts are proud to answer. On our inn pages we note it where the inn tells us, using terms like "free-flowing" or "some water added."

## Onsen with tattoos: what truly works

Tattoos remain the biggest hurdle at Japanese onsen. Many shared baths refuse guests with visible ink, a policy tied to tattoos’ historical association with organized crime. Our numbers show the reality: only [25 of our 2,598 ryokan](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?tattoos=fine) allow tattoos in shared baths, while [1,331 let you bathe privately](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?tattoos=private) in a bath in your room or a private bath you book.

For most travelers with tattoos, the solution is a private bath. You might cover small tattoos with a skin-colored patch, but the decision rests with the inn, not you. A few towns take a laid-back approach. Kinosaki’s seven public bathhouses permit tattoos of any size, and both of our Hakone selections below accept them, per their pages.

How to ask: an inn's tattoo policy often appears only on its Japanese website, if it's posted anywhere at all. When I book for friends, I send one brief message before paying, stating the tattoo's size and location, and ask which baths they're allowed to use. A direct question brings a direct reply, so no one feels embarrassed at the bath entrance. If you want the shortest list, we have [8 ryokan with private baths where tattoos are fine everywhere](https://ryokansociety.com/private-onsen?tattoos=fine).

## Best times to visit and soak duration

Onsen stay open all year, and winter is the season that suits them best, when snow surrounds the mountain outdoor baths. Autumn leaves rank second.

Japan's major holidays, New Year, Golden Week from late April to early May, and Obon in mid-August, bring crowds and higher prices to onsen towns. For a quieter, more affordable stay, pick a weeknight.

Begin with 5 to 10 minutes in the water, then step out to cool down before returning. Multiple short dips, two or three, work better than a single long soak. When you're at an inn, skip neither the bath after dinner nor the early-morning bath.

## Onsen in Japan FAQ

**Do you have to be nude to use an onsen?**
Yes, in shared baths. You don't wear a swimsuit and bring only a small towel, which stays outside the water. If that doesn't work for you, a [bath in your room](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?bath=room) or a private bath you book lets you soak alone or just with your group.

**Are onsen mixed gender?**
Nearly all onsen separate bathing areas by gender. You can still find mixed-gender baths, called konyoku, especially at old mountain onsen, and in many of these you're permitted to wear a bath wrap. Families and couples who'd like to bathe together usually reserve a [private bath](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?bath=private).

**How much does it cost to use an onsen?**
You'll pay a small fee to enter public bathhouses, and towels may cost extra. At a ryokan, access to the baths is included, and many small inns also include their private baths.

**Can children use an onsen?**
Yes. Young children usually enter the shared bath with one parent, but the age limit for entering with the other parent varies by location, so confirm with the front desk. Some ryokan have age restrictions for guests, so check the inn page or begin your search with [ryokan that welcome children](https://ryokansociety.com/ryokans?children=welcome).

**Can you use an onsen during your period?**
On heavy days, most guests skip the shared baths out of courtesy to other bathers. Having a bath in your room solves it.

**Are there any onsen near Tokyo?**
Two classic destinations are Hakone and Kusatsu, both home to dozens of ryokan with their own hot-spring baths. Explore our [ryokan near Tokyo](https://ryokansociety.com/near-tokyo) for more.

**Is day-only access available?**
Yes. You can visit onsen towns and use public bathhouses, and some ryokan allow day guests in their baths. But access to the night and morning baths is only for those staying overnight.
