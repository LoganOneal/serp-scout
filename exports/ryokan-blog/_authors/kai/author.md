Kai Sulkin learned what makes a great Japanese inn from the back of the house, not the brochure. He grew up in Tokyo in a hotel family.

Today Kai splits his time between Tokyo and New York City, and he founded Ryokan Society, the premier way for international travelers to discover and book standout ryokan in Japan.

## Both sides of the front desk

Living between the two cities, Kai sees the ryokan from both sides: how innkeepers work, and what international guests want to know before they book. Can we bathe privately? Will my tattoo be a problem? What exactly is dinner, and what time do we have to arrive for it?

Ryokan Society works with the inns themselves to get those answers right. Our editors also read each inn's Japanese-language pages, where the details usually live, and put everything in English, right beside the price.

## What he looks for in a ryokan

- **A small number of rooms, not a big number of amenities.** At fifteen rooms the staff learn your name and dinner is timed to you. That is omotenashi, anticipating what a guest needs before they ask, and it doesn't scale.
- **The kitchen.** Japanese cuisine is the part of the ryokan Kai cares about most: kaiseki built on what's in season, and in winter the snow crab of the Sea of Japan coast.
- **The water.** A private bath in your room is often heated tap water; a bath fed from the hot spring is the real thing. Our guides say which is which.
- **Rules you can see before you book:** tattoos, children's ages, dinner check-in times.
